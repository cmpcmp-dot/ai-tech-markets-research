Welcome to our AI dashboard.

We don’t know what this technology will become, how the economy will absorb it, or what policy should do in response. The hard part is that we have to answer the third before anyone answers the first two.

  1. Research. It’s unclear how the technology itself will take shape. Will it be a normal technology, as Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue, or is it more like electricity, where the whole economy eventually reorganizes around it?
  2. Economy. Even as the technology evolves, how the economy absorbs it is a separate question. Will AI’s effect on the labor market be sudden and large, or slower and more jagged as it works its way into existing firms and institutions?
  3. Policy. And even then, it’s hard to know how to react. What policies safeguard against the downsides, amplify what’s good, and make sure this broadly helps people? Should we work through the policies we already have, or does a genuinely new era demand bigger and different ideas? How much can we steer the technology, and how much should we just plan on absorbing its effects?

Each question calls for a different kind of answer, which is how this dashboard is organized. We’re learning here alongside everyone else, but we think what we’ve built can contribute to a better AI transition.

Research

Our research tracker is a searchable, tagged list of work on AI and its impact, broadly defined. Alongside it is our fact bank, meant as a resource for the community as a whole: specific sentences you can consider using, each with a caveat section laying out what the finding actually supports and where it has been stretched.

How this section is built. To cover this much ground, the tracker and fact bank are summarized with the help of AI and then human review. We’ve built skills to check the output for accuracy, but as with any secondhand source, verify anything you plan to cite or build on. Terminal-based AI tools are becoming more important for ideas-based work, and we’d rather experiment with putting them into our own workflow than only write about them.

Economy

The first section is a real-time data tracker that pulls monthly and quarterly values from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), and other government sources. Rather than waiting on long-term forecasts, it provides immediate, accessible feedback on the exact macroeconomic metrics we are watching as the technology rolls out.

The second section tracks how AI is showing up in the labor market. Rather than running complicated statistical tests, we tell a story about the different ways you might look at this, to give a broader sense of what is and isn’t moving. We update it with each new data release, and we show the range of tools economists are bringing to the question.

There will be no jobs day where AI’s effect on the labor market becomes undeniable and forces a policy response. In that way it’s less like 9/11 or the COVID lockdowns and more like climate change: the impact accumulates without ever announcing itself. We hope this makes it easier to follow while we’re still early.

Policy

Our policy map includes nearly every idea people have raised for making AI safe and broadly beneficial. Click through any of them and you’ll find the major bills and initiatives from that policy’s advocates. If you’re trying to compile what has actually been introduced, start here.

This is also where we build on our theory of provoking and legitimizing policy solutions. In a world where AI is fundamentally disruptive, the work is provocation: ideas that go well beyond shoring up what we already have. In a world where AI mostly accelerates the prosperity and the inequality of the last several decades, the work is legitimation, and that’s the harder job. We already know what those solutions look like. They have been contested for thirty years.
