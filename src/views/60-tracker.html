What the data said this month.

Five sources, read on release day. Short, current, and regenerated from the same pipeline that produces everything else on this site. This is the monitoring view; the longer arguments that use these series live under Job Displacement.

Jobs

Is the labour market loosening?

The headline unemployment rate, what it looks like against the rate the CBO thinks is sustainable, and whether prime-age workers are still attached to the labour force.

Prime-age participation and employment
Ages 25–54, monthly, seasonally adjusted

Prime-age attachment is the number to watch, and it has not broken. Participation is above where it was in February 2020; the employment rate is a touch below. Neither is what a labour market absorbing a displacement shock looks like.

Payroll employment by AI-adoption group
January 2019 = 100, seasonally adjusted

Splitting payrolls by how much AI an industry reports using is the crudest version of the displacement test, and it is worth being clear that it identifies nothing. Industries that adopted AI early differ from industries that did not in size, geography, education mix and exposure to the post-2021 tech correction. The Job Displacement tab takes the question up properly, including how large an effect a design like this could actually detect.

JOLTS

Are firms still hiring?

Displacement, if it is happening, shows up in flows before it shows up in the unemployment rate. A firm that stops backfilling roles never lays anyone off. These are the four flows, split by how much AI the industry reports using.

Hiring has cooled across the board, and it cooled in low-adoption industries too. Read the gap between the lines, not the level of any one of them, and read it as description rather than as an effect.

What these series are, and are not

This is the adoption split, not the national print. The published contract carries JOLTS rates grouped into thirds by industry AI adoption, because that is what the displacement analysis needs. The national headline rates that lead the JOLTS release are a separate, unbuilt step; until it exists this tab does not show them.

Rates, not levels. Every series is a share of employment, so groups of very different size are comparable. Openings are a share of employment plus openings, which is the JOLTS convention and slightly different from the others.

The grouping is fixed, the outcome moves. Industries are assigned to a group once, using adoption measured before the November 2025 BTOS question change, so the split does not shift underneath the series.

GDP

Is growth fast enough to hold unemployment down?

Okun's law gives a growth rate that keeps unemployment flat. Below it, unemployment tends to rise. It is a regularity rather than a law, and it is the cheapest way to ask whether the labour market has a growth problem or something else.

Real GDP growth
Annualised quarterly rate, 2015 onward

Growth running under breakeven with unemployment not rising is the interesting configuration, and it is roughly where we are. That combination is what a productivity story would look like, and it is also what measurement noise looks like. The Okun residual is small either way.

The fit is estimated over the full postwar sample with the two pandemic quarters excluded, because leaving them in lets 2020 set the slope for eighty years of data. The R² is what it always is for Okun's law: high enough to be useful, low enough that you should not read a single quarter's residual as news.

Who is actually adopting AI, what are they using it for, and is anyone losing a job over it?

Most of the AI-and-work debate runs on forecasts. The Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey asks roughly a million businesses, every two weeks, what they are actually doing. The answer is a chain: firms are adopting, exposure predicts which ones, the work is language work — and the last link, from adoption to job loss, is the one that does not hold.

The aggregate

How many firms are we talking about?

Census asks the same question every two weeks. In November 2025 the wording changed, so the series is drawn as two segments and never spliced.

Share of U.S. businesses using AI, and expecting to
Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey, biweekly, firm-weighted

About one firm in five, rising steadily — and four in five still report no AI use at all. Both readings are true, and the gap between them is most of the argument about what this technology is doing to work.

Adoption is rising fast in proportional terms: from under four percent to over twenty in under three years, on a base of roughly a million surveyed businesses. The level is still low. Anyone who tells you the number is dramatic and anyone who tells you it is trivial is reading the same chart.

The break matters for how you quote it. In November 2025 the question moved from AI use “in producing goods or services” to AI use “in any business function,” and the level jumped with it. The rewording also coincided with a six-week collection gap during the funding lapse, which is left open rather than interpolated. The two solid segments answer different questions and must not be joined into one series.

The dashed line is the share of firms that expect to be using AI six months out. It runs above the solid line almost everywhere, which is the ordinary pattern for stated intentions in survey data and a reason to treat forward-looking AI numbers — including forward-looking claims about headcount — as an upper bound rather than a forecast.

Exposure

Does the theory predict the practice?

Occupational exposure scores were predictions about a world that had not happened yet. Plotted against what firms report actually doing, industry by industry.

BTOS adoption against employment-weighted occupational exposure
48 three-digit NAICS industries · adoption is the pre-break mean, Sep 2023 – Nov 2025 · exposure is the PCA composite, z-scored
Each point is an industry. The fitted line is ordinary least squares on the points shown. Hover for the industry code, its exposure in standard deviations, and its adoption rate. Points labeled where the industry is recognizable: 518 data processing and hosting, 541 professional and technical services, 511 publishing, 722 food service, 484 trucking.

Yes. Industries whose workers hold more exposed occupations adopt AI substantially more, and the relationship holds under every exposure measure in the literature. The exposure research is measuring something firms act on.

The design is Tucker's, not ours

This chart and the method behind it come from Tucker (2025), who first regressed BTOS industry adoption on occupational AI exposure and reported roughly 6.7 percentage points of adoption per standard deviation of exposure, with an R² of about 0.47 at the subsector level, using crosswalked Eloundou GPT-4 exposure. What follows is a replication of that design on different inputs, not an independent discovery — and the value of running it again is that it tests how much the answer depends on which exposure measure you feed it.

Our version substitutes the Budget Lab at Yale's replication of the exposure literature and repeats the regression across all seven of its measures. Each occupation's score is collapsed to a 3-digit industry using CPS occupation-by-industry employment weights, so an industry counts as exposed to the extent that the people working in it hold exposed jobs. That industry exposure is then matched to the industry's average BTOS adoption rate over the consistent pre-rewording span.

The gradient lands close to where Tucker put it: 6.7 points per standard deviation there, inside the confidence interval here. Different exposure inputs, a different adoption window, roughly the same slope — which is the useful result, because it means the finding is not an artifact of one team's choice of exposure score.

Robustness across exposure measures

The composite is a weighted average of the underlying scores, and it is worth knowing whether the result depends on which one you pick. It does not. Every metric in the literature, including Microsoft's realized-usage measure built from a completely different kind of data, produces a positive and statistically significant slope.

Slope in percentage points of adoption per standard deviation of exposure, HC3 robust standard errors, n = 48 industries throughout. Stars: *** p<0.01, ** p<0.05, * p<0.1. The composite is the PCA-variance-weighted average of the others after z-scoring; Webb's patent-based measure is excluded because its PCA weight is an order of magnitude smaller.
Who

Which firms?

The national average hides almost everything interesting. Size is the gradient that never breaks.

Adoption by employment size class, over time
Firm-weighted within each size class, segmented at the rewording

Large firms, in information, professional services and education. Industry has the wider spread; firm size is the more consistent gradient, and the one with the biggest interpretive consequence — because adopters are large employers, the share of workers at an AI-using firm runs well ahead of the firm-weighted headline.

Big employers adopt more than small ones in every period, on both sides of the wording change, and in the separate AI supplement. BTOS weights by firm, and most firms are small. Because adopters are disproportionately large employers, the share of workers at an AI-using employer runs well above the share of firms using AI. Census publishes no employment-weighted headline, so we show the gradient rather than assert a number.

What for

What is AI actually doing inside these firms?

The question the aggregate adoption rate cannot answer, and the one that determines whether adoption should displace anyone.

Which business functions use AI
Share of all businesses, multiple responses allowed, AI supplement

Language work at the commercial edge of the firm: drafting, summarizing, searching, marketing. These are tasks, and mostly tasks that sit inside jobs rather than constituting them. And the main thing holding back the non-adopters is perceived relevance, not cost, regulation or workers refusing.

AI is entering through the commercial front of the business — marketing, strategy, IT — not through production or logistics. That is close to the opposite of the pattern industrial robots followed, and it points the labor-market effect at white-collar task content rather than at manufacturing headcount.

What generative AI is used for
Among firms whose employees use generative AI, multiple responses allowed

Writing and editing dominates, then search and summarization. A displacement story has to explain how a firm gets from “our staff draft emails faster” to “we need fewer staff,” and the survey does not show that step happening yet.

Why the other four in five say no

The barriers question is the most underrated series in the supplement, because it tells you what would have to change for diffusion to continue.

Reasons firms give for not planning to use AI
Among firms not planning to adopt within six months, multiple responses allowed

Not cost. Not regulation. Not workers refusing. Overwhelmingly relevance — firms say AI does not apply to what they do — followed by not knowing how to use it. Both are ceilings that fall as capabilities broaden and knowledge spreads, which is one reason to expect diffusion to keep going rather than to plateau here.

Where

Where are the adopters?

Firm-weighted AI use by state, with the 25 largest metro areas on top. Darker is more.

AI use by state and metro area
Latest collection period, firm-weighted, share of businesses reporting AI use

Adoption is highest in the Northeast corridor, the Mountain West and Florida, and lowest across the Plains and the Deep South. The metro layer tracks the states it sits in more than it stands apart from them.

The state range runs from the high twenties down to about eleven percent, so the spread is real but narrow next to the size and industry gradients — a factor of roughly two-and-a-half across states, against a factor of several across sectors. Much of what does vary looks like industry composition: places with more information and professional-services firms sit higher.

Hover any state or metro for its estimate and standard error. These are state-level subsamples of a firm survey, so individual estimates carry meaningful sampling error — treat the broad pattern as more reliable than any single state's rank. Alaska and Rhode Island are suppressed by Census. Ten small states plus DC are unreadable at this scale, and Puerto Rico has an estimate but no polygon in the projection, so all of them appear as labelled squares below the map.

And the jobs

Do adopters say it cost anyone a job?

The supplement asks firms using AI what it did to their total employment — the closest thing to a direct measure of AI-attributed job loss in official U.S. statistics.

Self-reported effect of AI on total employment
Among businesses using AI, firm-weighted

Essentially no — and this is the link that does not hold. The overwhelming majority of AI-using firms report no employment change at all, and slightly more report an increase than a decrease. But the question cannot see forgone hiring, which is exactly where the displacement literature says to look. Read it as a bound on attributed layoffs, not a verdict on displacement.

Take the caveats seriously, because they are load-bearing. Firms have every reason not to attribute a layoff to AI, and often no clean way to know. More importantly, the question asks about employment, and the mechanism most of the microdata literature actually finds is not firing — it is not hiring. A firm that quietly stops backfilling entry-level roles reports no decrease here, and its payroll count drifts down through ordinary attrition without a single layoff.

It is also worth sitting with the sign. Slightly more adopters say AI raised their headcount than say it cut it. That is not evidence AI creates jobs — the numbers are small and self-reported either way — but it is inconsistent with the version of the story in which adoption and layoffs move together, and it is what you would expect if AI is currently making existing workers more productive at tasks they already do.

That is the shape of the evidence as it stands: a genuine, accelerating, exposure-predicted diffusion of AI into firms, doing language tasks at the commercial edge of the business, with no visible layoff signature attached to it. If displacement is happening, it is happening through the hiring door.

Which is a claim about flows, not stocks, and it needs BLS data rather than survey self-reports to test. The Job Displacement tab takes it up: whether higher-adoption industries are hiring less, whether they are laying off more, and how large an effect this design could actually detect if it were there.
What this data can and cannot tell you

Everything here is firm-weighted. BTOS weights to the population of businesses, not workers. Since adoption rises steeply with firm size, every level on this page understates worker exposure. Census publishes no employment-weighted figure and we do not construct one; the size gradient under Who is the honest substitute.

The November 2025 rewording is a real break. The question moved from AI use in producing goods or services to AI use in any business function, and the level moved with it. The two segments are never spliced, and the six-week shutdown gap is left open rather than interpolated. A handful of later periods re-ask the retired wording; those rows are kept in the tidy data and excluded from every chart, because they belong to neither series.

Suppressed cells are shown, not dropped. Census suppresses small cells; those bars appear at reduced opacity and marked with a dagger so that a thin industry is visibly thin rather than silently missing. On the map, states with no estimate are drawn in a flat neutral tone and listed in the legend.

Percentages are rounded. Values of ten percent and above are shown as whole numbers; below ten they keep one decimal, because at that end rounding would erase real differences — the gap between the share of adopters reporting an employment increase and the share reporting a decrease is the case that matters.

Geography is measured imprecisely. State and metro figures are subsamples of a firm survey and carry standard errors of a few percentage points, which is large relative to the spread across states. The map shows the levels as published; it does not test whether any two places differ.

The exposure join is the least standard thing on this page. The design is Tucker's (2025). Occupation scores come from the Budget Lab at Yale's replication of the exposure literature, aggregated to industry with CPS 2021–22 occupation-by-industry employment weights. About ten percent of employment cannot be mapped, mostly construction, which CPS codes as a single industry, and public administration. Using 2023–24 weights instead correlates at 0.99, so the choice of weight window does not drive the result.

Adoption is not causation. Nothing on this page identifies an effect of AI on anything. Industries that adopt AI differ from industries that do not in many ways, and the exposure gradient is a correlation between two industry characteristics.
