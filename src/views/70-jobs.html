Is AI displacing workers yet?

The headline unemployment rate says one thing. Look closer and it says something more specific. Five questions, asked of the labor-market data as it is released.

The short version

01 The level

Unemployment is , at or below CBO’s natural rate, and its path tracks growth about as Okun’s law predicts.

Is unemployment higher than growth alone would predict, and is a low rate hiding people who have stopped looking?

Okun’s law: GDP growth vs. change in unemployment

Okun’s law ties the change in unemployment to how fast the economy grows: strong GDP growth pulls unemployment down, weak growth pushes it up. If unemployment were rising faster than growth predicts, that would point to something structural — a displacement effect layered on the ordinary business cycle. Each point is a quarter; the line is the historical relationship, and the last four quarters are traced in sequence.

They sit close to it. Unemployment can also mislead if people stop looking for work, since they leave the labor force and the rate falls even as the market weakens. The companion check is prime-age participation, which strips out retirement.

Prime-age (25–54) labor force participation & employment rate
Monthly, seasonally adjusted, since 2000

What this cannot establish

This is the coarsest measure on the page and the last place displacement would appear. The Okun relationship leaves most quarter-to-quarter variation unexplained, so only a very large shock would clear the noise, and it is fitted on eighty years in which nothing resembling AI displacement occurs. An effect concentrated in one cohort or one sector — which is what the next two sections report — would leave no trace here at all. Read this as ruling out a macroeconomic event, not as evidence of calm.

02 Across workers

The weakness is concentrated: young graduates carry more unemployment than their own pre-2020 norm predicts.

Is the weakness spread evenly across workers, or is one group absorbing it?

How to read this section

Every group of workers has its own settled relationship to the national unemployment rate. When the economy weakens, unemployment rises faster for the young than for the old, and faster for workers without degrees than for those with them. Those ratios are stable enough to be useful: tell us the national rate and we can say what a given group’s rate ought to be.

So that is what we do. For each combination of age and education we learn that relationship from the fifteen years before the pandemic, then ask what the group’s unemployment rate should be today, given where the national rate actually is. All three charts below plot the same thing: the gap between that prediction and reality. Zero means a group is exactly where its own history says it should be. Positive means it is doing worse than its own track record predicts.

One extra step matters, because it changes the numbers. We tested whether the method returns zero when it should, by hiding the recent past and asking it to predict years we already know were unremarkable. It came back positive, by roughly half a point, so the procedure runs hot. That amount is subtracted from every figure on this page. What is left is the part the method cannot explain away as its own habit.

What each group has, against what the national rate predicts

What this chart does. It puts the two unemployment rates side by side for six groups. The hollow dot is the rate predicted from the national figure; the filled dot is the rate the group actually has. The bar between them is the gap. Read the lengths of the bars rather than their positions: one is conspicuously longer than the rest.

The same gap, across the whole age range

What this chart does. It takes the gap from the first chart and traces it across every five-year age band from the early twenties to the mid-sixties, so you can see where in the age range it sits rather than just that it exists. The grey band is the room the method’s own bias creates: a line inside it is not evidence of anything. Dot size is sample size, which is why the youngest graduate points are drawn smallest.

When the gap opened up

What this chart does. It answers the question the first two cannot: whether this is new. It follows the gap for young graduates month by month back to 2005. The grey band is the entire range this measure occupied from 2005 through 2019, the Great Recession included, so anything above the band is territory the pre-pandemic era never reached, in good years or bad.

The shape is the finding, and it is narrower than a story about young workers. Non-graduates in their twenties sit at essentially zero, and so do graduates past forty-five. What separates the affected group from the unaffected ones is not age alone and not education alone, but the combination: people at the start of a career that a degree was supposed to open.

The timing matters as much as the shape. This gap did not appear with the pandemic and then linger. It closed to almost nothing by early 2023 and has widened in most months since, which is the opposite of what a fading shock looks like.

What this cannot establish

Concentration, not cause. The same pattern would appear if firms had cut graduate training programs for ordinary cost reasons, or if degree supply had outrun demand in particular fields. The most serious alternative is compositional: as the share of young people finishing degrees rises, the average graduate is drawn from further down the distribution, which lifts the group’s unemployment rate relative to the national rate with no change in employer behaviour at all. Nothing here separates that from a demand story. The entry-level market was also weakening before generative AI was deployed at scale. Postings data by occupational exposure is what would distinguish these, and it is not in this tracker yet.

The technical specification
Data
IPUMS CPS basic monthly, , persons 16+, weighted by WTFINL. Unemployed is EMPSTAT ∈ {20,21,22}; the labor force is LABFORCE == 2. College+ is EDUC ≥ 111; HS+ (no BA) is 73 ≤ EDUC < 111.
Estimator
For each band g, ordinary least squares on months through :
log( rg,t ) = αg + βg · log( Rt ) + εg,t
where rg,t is the band’s trailing 12-month mean unemployment rate and Rt the same for all workers 16+. The fitted value is back-transformed as exp(·), so it targets the conditional median rather than the mean. β is a cyclical elasticity: below one means the band is less cycle-sensitive than the aggregate.
Statistic
The mean of (actual − predicted) over the latest months, in rate units, then less the placebo bias below. Because the fit is multiplicative, a gap in percentage points scales with the band’s base rate, so the actual-to-predicted ratio is the like-for-like comparison across groups; both are in the tooltips.
Bands
Five single years of age wide, plotted at the centre. The lower edge of the youngest band is pinned at 21 for College+ and 18 for HS+ (no BA), so no band spans an age at which the credential is effectively unattainable. < HS is excluded: below 21 that category is defined by an outcome that is mechanically age-dependent, and its share of the young population more than halved over the sample, so its residuals track who remains in the category rather than labor demand.
Placebo correction
The same procedure re-run with the training cut moved back, then evaluated over the two following years, which were ordinary:
    Mean error is almost identical to mean absolute error in the original diagnostics, so the miss is one-directional across nearly every band: bias, not noise. The mechanism is that the aggregate rate is not a sufficient statistic for a group’s rate, because band-level β drifts as educational composition shifts, so a falling aggregate makes the fit over-predict improvement. The correction is estimated per band and subtracted. It matters most for comparisons between groups, since HS+ carries the larger bias.
    Moving average
    Taken over 12 calendar months, requiring at least 11 observed. October 2025 CPS was never collected, so a positional 12-row average would have silently spanned 13 calendar months for every window in the current reporting period.
    Diagnostics
    Known limits
    No standard errors are placed on the gap itself; the placebo band is what stands in for them, and it is estimated from two scenarios only, so the headline is better read as a range than a point. The youngest graduate cells are thin. Each point is a 12-month average and the window closes in , so this is a slow-moving object describing roughly the year to then, not a current-month reading.

    03 Across industries

    The industries using AI most have added jobs fastest: the high-adoption third stands at against for the low-adoption third, on 2019 = 100.

    If AI displaces workers, the industries buying it should be the ones shedding jobs. Are they?

    How to read sections 03 and 04

    The Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey asks roughly a million businesses, every two weeks, whether they used AI to produce goods or services. Averaging each industry’s answers gives one number per industry: the share of its firms using AI. Sort industries by that number, cut them into three equal groups, and you have the high, middle and low adoption groups plotted below.

    Then ignore the survey entirely and watch what BLS reports for those groups. Every line in these two sections is payroll or turnover data. Adoption is used only to decide which industries go in which group, which is what makes this a descriptive cut rather than an estimate of anything.

    Two features of the measure matter throughout. It counts firms, not workers, and adopters skew large, so the share of employees inside an AI-using firm runs well above these figures. And the survey rewrote the question in November 2025, so the grouping uses only the earlier, consistent wording.

    Employment by AI-adoption group, indexed to 2019

    What this chart does. It stacks the three groups on a common base, so the vertical distance between lines is the difference in growth since 2019, not in size. The displacement hypothesis predicts the high-adoption line ends up lowest. It ends up highest, and has done throughout.

    Which sectors are in each group

    This is the cut most likely to be screenshotted, in either direction, so it is worth being clear about what it is not. It is not evidence that AI creates jobs. The ordering here is mostly a statement about which industries were growing before anyone typed a prompt, and about which ones the pandemic knocked down hardest.

    What it does establish is a floor for the argument. Any claim that AI is visibly subtracting jobs at the industry level has to explain why the industries doing the adopting are the ones that grew fastest, and it has to do that with something more than the raw series.

    What this cannot establish

    Almost nothing, on its own. The gap that opens in 2020–22 is pandemic composition rather than AI: the low-adoption group is accommodation and food services, construction and transportation, which fell hardest in 2020 and were still climbing back into 2024. Adoption is also not randomly assigned, and the causation plausibly runs backwards, since profitable growing firms have the slack to buy new technology. Eighteen sectors is a coarse grid, and BTOS counts firms while CES counts jobs, so the two sides of this comparison are weighted differently.

    The technical specification
    Treatment
    Each sector’s mean BTOS “currently using AI” share, firm-weighted, over collection periods before the survey rewrote the question. Sectors are cut at the terciles of that distribution, six per group.
    Outcome
    BLS Current Employment Statistics, all employees, seasonally adjusted. Employment is summed within each group and indexed to the group’s own 2019 mean, so the lines carry growth rather than level.
    Sample
    Known limits
    Firm-weighted treatment against a worker-weighted outcome. No controls of any kind: not for pre-trend, not for pandemic catch-up, not for industry composition. With eighteen sectors there is no power to detect a modest effect, so the absence of a negative gradient here is weak evidence of absence rather than strong evidence of nothing.
    Would change this The high-adoption index falling below the low-adoption index, or the gap narrowing once each sector’s 2017–19 trend and its distance from the 2019 level are conditioned out.
    04 Hiring and firing

    Mostly a freeze rather than a purge. Hires and quits are below their 2019 levels in every group and furthest below where adoption is highest — but that group is also the only one whose layoffs rate, at , is back above where it started.

    A payroll count nets hiring against separations. Which side of that is moving, and does it move differently where AI is used?

    Openings, hires, quits and layoffs by AI-adoption group
    JOLTS rates, three-month moving average, employment-weighted within each group

    What this chart does. It separates the two things a payroll number adds together. Openings and hires are demand for new workers; quits are workers’ own confidence; layoffs are firms shedding staff. Displacement by automation has a signature: hiring falls, layoffs stay flat, and the gap opens where the technology is used. Read the levels against the 2019 stretch on the left of each panel, and read the distance between the lines within each panel.

    Which supersectors are in each group

    Most of that is the low-hire, low-fire economy everyone has been describing, and it is not specific to AI: the three groups turn together, and openings have round-tripped to roughly where they were in 2019 across all of them. The exception is the last panel. Layoffs are the one series where the ordering runs the way a displacement story predicts, and the high-adoption group is the only one that has not returned to its 2019 rate.

    What this cannot establish

    Read the layoffs panel with its composition in mind. The largest employer in the high-adoption group is professional and business services, which JOLTS reports as one supersector pooling NAICS 54, 55 and 56 — and 56 contains temporary help services, the most volatile employment in the survey and a sector that has been shedding jobs since 2022 for reasons that long predate generative AI. A tenth of a percentage point spread across five supersectors is not a finding that can be pinned on anything.

    More generally: fifteen supersectors, five per group, is the coarsest grid on this page, and these units are broader than the CES sectors in section 03. A displacement effect concentrated in one occupation inside one of them would be invisible. These are also rates, not counts, so a group whose employment base is growing can post a falling hires rate while hiring more people — which, given section 03, is exactly what the high-adoption group has been doing.

    The technical specification
    Treatment
    The same pre-break BTOS adoption measure as section 03, mapped from BTOS sectors to JOLTS supersectors by analysis/src/lookups/crosswalk_btos_jolts.csv, then cut at terciles, five supersectors per group.
    Outcome
    BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, seasonally adjusted rates. Within a group, rates are averaged across supersectors weighted by employment, then smoothed with a three-month trailing mean.
    Sample
    Scale
    Each panel is free-scaled, so vertical distances are not comparable between panels. The layoffs panel is clipped: its three-month average peaks near ten percent in spring 2020, and at full scale the entire post-2021 range would be a few pixels tall. The clip level, the true peaks and the exit points are all marked; the figures are in the chart note.
    Known limits
    Same firm-weighted-treatment problem as section 03. Openings are a percent of employment plus openings and so are not on the same denominator as the other three. Nothing here is conditioned on anything.
    Would change this The high-adoption layoffs gap surviving the removal of professional and business services from that group, which would rule out temporary help as the explanation. Or the gap widening beyond a few tenths of a point, which is where it stops being noise across five supersectors.
    05 Early careers

    One test does find something: early-career hiring is weaker per standard deviation of industry adoption. Then the timing undoes most of the AI reading of it.

    Sections 03 and 04 compare industries in aggregate. What happens with a sharper instrument, one that compares young and prime-age workers inside the same industry?

    How to read this section

    The problem with every comparison above is that industries differ for a hundred reasons besides AI. This test removes all of them at once. For each industry it takes the change in hiring of 22 to 24 year olds and subtracts the change in hiring of 35 to 44 year olds in that same industry. Anything that hit the whole industry, a demand collapse or a post-pandemic correction, drops out of the difference.

    What is left is whether young workers specifically did worse in the industries that adopted AI. The data is the Census LEHD Quarterly Workforce Indicators, which counts actual hires by age from state unemployment insurance records rather than from a survey.

    Change in hiring, 22–24 minus 35–44, against adoption

    What this chart does. Each dot is a subsector. Moving right means more AI adoption; moving down means early-career hiring fell further than prime-age hiring in that same industry. The line slopes down, and it is the most precisely estimated relationship on this page.

    That result compares two windows, 2019 against the most recent four quarters. It cannot say when the gap opened, and a two-window comparison cannot tell a gap that opened the month ChatGPT shipped from one that opened in April 2020 and never closed. So run the same regression once per quarter against the same 2019 base.

    The same coefficient, estimated every quarter

    What this chart does. It replaces one number with its history. Flat through 2019 is the pre-trend, and it is the part that supports the design. Then watch where the drop happens relative to the purple line.

    So the cross-section is real and the AI reading of it is much narrower than it looks. The gap between young and prime-age hiring in high-adoption industries is a pandemic artifact that never closed, with a further leg since 2023 that is consistent with AI diffusion and equally consistent with the tech correction, elevated interest rates, or the same industries simply continuing what they started in 2020.

    What this cannot establish

    Adoption is measured at the industry level and outcomes at the industry level, so this is not the firm-level test the displacement literature actually needs. No public source links firm-level AI adoption to worker-level outcomes. The strongest objection to the cross-section survives the age difference: industries that adopted AI early are more white-collar, more urban, and were more exposed to the post-2021 tech correction, and the age difference removes what hit those industries uniformly, not something that hit their young workers specifically.

    The technical specification
    Data
    LEHD Quarterly Workforce Indicators, all private ownership, hires and employment by NAICS subsector and age band, joined to pre-break BTOS adoption at the subsector level. Young is QWI age band A03 (22–24), prime-age is A05 (35–44). Hires are trailing four-quarter sums, which removes seasonality without an adjustment model.
    Estimator
    Cross-sectional OLS across subsectors of the age-differenced change in log hires on standardised adoption:
    Δlog Hi,22-24 − Δlog Hi,35-44 = α + β · z(adoptioni) + εi
    The event study is this same regression re-estimated once per quarter against a fixed 2019 base. At the final quarter it reproduces the cross-section, and the build asserts that equality.

    Known limits
    Industry-level treatment, industry-level outcome. No standard errors are clustered beyond the cross-section, and with 59 subsectors the leverage of any single one is non-trivial. Coverage of the QWI panel is incomplete in the most recent quarters, so the last point moves as states report.
    Would change this The quarterly coefficient continuing to fall through 2026 while the pandemic leg stays flat, which would put the weight of the effect in the AI era rather than in 2020. Or firm-level evidence, which would replace this test rather than adjust it.
