01The number on the page today comes from a file no code produces

The live figure is drawn from age_diff_sa_3lines.csv, downloaded from the blog repo. That file has three problems, and they compound.

  • It is an orphan. The current 01_big_graphic_YoY.R writes age_diff_3lines.csv and age_diff.csv. Nothing in that repo still produces the _sa variant. It is residue from a code path that was deleted. There is also no seasonal-adjustment step anywhere in the repo, so the _sa label is itself wrong.
  • It is stale. Committed , roughly six months before this run.
  • Its headline band spans ages . The peak point is the College+ band centred at 21, which includes 19 and 20 year olds holding bachelor's degrees. That is a selection artifact, not a cohort.

Porting the current source script reproduces it to within about 0.3 pp for College+, so the method transferred correctly. The gap to the live site is the orphan file, not the port.

02Six defects, and what each one was doing

03The measure was reporting excess unemployment when nothing was happening

This is the substantive finding, and it was already sitting in the source repo. Move the training cut back, then measure the residual over the two years that follow, years in which nothing happened to these cohorts. The procedure should return roughly zero. It does not.

Mean error is almost exactly equal to mean absolute error, so the miss runs one way across essentially every band. That is bias, not noise. The mechanism is that the aggregate unemployment rate is not a sufficient statistic for a group's rate: group betas drift as educational composition shifts, so in any stretch where the aggregate is falling, the fit over-predicts improvement for these groups.

The rebuild re-estimates this per band on current data and subtracts it. The correction matters most for the comparison between groups, because HS+ carries a larger bias than College+, so an unadjusted comparison in percentage points was never like-for-like.

04What the numbers look like fixed

The cleanup sharpens the finding rather than weakening it. The old version showed young non-graduates elevated as well, which muddied the claim about the young and the educated. Almost all of that was the method's own bias plus boundary noise. Adjusted, non-graduates sit at roughly zero at every age. The excess belongs to young graduates specifically, not to young workers generally.

The specification check is strong. Over , a stretch that includes the Great Recession, the adjusted residual for young graduates never rose above . It is now, and it has been outside that entire range every month since ( consecutive months).

05Is this the right way to understand youth unemployment?

It answers one narrow question well: conditional on the aggregate rate, is this group unusual? It is not a good way to understand youth unemployment, and the page should not imply otherwise.

  1. Composition is the threat worth emphasising above all others. Bachelor's attainment has risen substantially across this sample, so "College+ 22–27" in 2026 is a larger, less selected slice of the cohort than it was in 2010, drawn further down the distribution. That alone raises the group's unemployment rate relative to the aggregate with no change in labour demand at all. This method cannot separate the two, and a slow monotone drift since 2023 is exactly what gradual composition change looks like. Until that is ruled out, this is not an AI story or even a labour-demand story.
  2. It is purely relative, and reads as more alarming than the level warrants. Young-graduate unemployment is . It reached in the Great Recession aftermath and at the pandemic peak. What is unusual is the relationship to a aggregate, not the level.
  3. The unemployment rate is the wrong margin for young workers. Entry-level weakness surfaces first in underemployment, graduates holding jobs that never required a degree, and in enrolment and participation. A graduate who takes a barista job is employed. This measure is silent on the part that matters most and probably understates the problem. The New York Fed underemployment series is the natural complement, and the blog folder already carries nyfed_young_BA_unrate.csv.
  4. Graduate school is a countercyclical substitute for job search. Weak demand pushes marginal graduates out of the labour force entirely, which flatters the rate.

06Caveats that belong on the page

  • It lags badly. Each point is a 12-month average and the window is the latest months, so the figure describes roughly to , centred around late 2025. It should not be labelled latest data.
  • The bias adjustment is itself uncertain. Two placebo scenarios per band, and across the young graduate bands the estimate ranges from to . Quote the headline as a range of roughly , not a point estimate.
  • The youngest graduate cells are thin. About people in the labour force per month in the band.
  • HS+ (no BA) is noisy. The two parameterisations correlate for College+ but only for HS+. That is noise around zero rather than a dispute about shape, but no structure should be read into that line.
  • The fit is interpolating, not extrapolating, which is the good case: the current 12-month-average aggregate rate of sits inside the training range.
  • It cannot establish cause. The page already says this well.
